Quantum re-encryption mandates

A briefing for CTOs and CIOs on the federal post-quantum deadlines, the agent control plane and board oversight

John Fisher · 2026

For US federal agencies, and soon for federal contractors, the move to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) now has compliance deadlines.1,2 Federal law and executive orders require agencies to replace quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms, such as RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, with quantum-resistant ones, and critical infrastructure operators are being helped, though not yet required, to plan the same move.3,1 This briefing also covers research on corporate readiness, what the deadlines mean for the agent control plane, and how AI is shortening the time from a cryptographic weakness to a working attack.

1. Federal policy beyond NSM-10

White House National Security Memorandum 10 (NSM-10) set the first federal strategy for post-quantum readiness in May 2022, with a goal of mitigating as much quantum risk as feasible by 2035, and required agencies to inventory vulnerable systems.4 The instrument that now governs federal systems is Executive Order 14412, “Securing the Nation Against Advanced Cryptographic Attacks,” signed on June 22, 2026 and implemented through OMB Memorandum M-26-15.1,2 It builds on the post-quantum standards that the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) released on August 13, 2024: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) for key establishment, and FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) for digital signatures.5

2. The harvest-now, decrypt-later threat

The harvest-now, decrypt-later (HNDL) threat is the reason to fund PQC now. US government sources warn that adversaries, possibly including state-sponsored groups, may already be intercepting and storing high-value encrypted enterprise traffic.1 Classical computers cannot decrypt it, but it costs little to keep, and it can be decrypted once a cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) is built. Any data that must stay confidential into the 2030s is therefore already at risk if it is captured today under classical public-key cryptography alone.1

3. Compliance deadlines and milestones

Executive Order 14412 of June 22, 2026 and OMB Memorandum M-26-15 set deadlines for federal high-value assets and high-impact systems, excluding National Security Systems.1,2 The order also directs the FAR Council to propose, within 180 days, a rule extending the requirements to covered federal contractors.1,6

December 31, 2030Federal agencies use PQC for key establishment in all high-value assets and high-impact systems.1,2Network handshakes, Transport Layer Security (TLS), session setup and other protocols for data in transit
December 31, 2031Federal agencies use PQC for digital signatures in the same systems.1,2Code signing, identity verification tokens, software supply chain updates, firmware validation and non-repudiation systems
By 2035NSA intends all National Security Systems to be quantum-resistant, OMB expects the remaining federal systems to be migrated, and NIST’s draft guidance would disallow RSA and elliptic-curve algorithms after 2035.4,7,2,8End state for classical public-key algorithms across federal systems

4. Supply chain effects and the agent control plane

The deadlines bind federal agencies now, and covered contractors will be bound once the FAR rule is final.1,2 That rule would require covered contractors to comply with NIST’s standards, including the post-quantum algorithms, by December 31, 2030, but by mid-October 2026 none had been proposed, and the order does not define covered contractors.1,6 Enterprises that sell software, cloud infrastructure (SaaS, PaaS or IaaS) or managed services to the US government should plan for the 2030 date. Which providers are covered, and how they show compliance, will be settled in the rulemaking.

The agent control plane belongs early in the inventory and the migration. Agents exchange instructions and data with tools, models and other agents across clouds and services, over encrypted connections such as TLS. Any of that traffic protected only by classical key exchange can be recorded now and decrypted later, and NIST expects key establishment to move first, particularly in interactive protocols such as TLS.8 OMB’s memorandum already expects infrastructure that terminates TLS to support post-quantum key exchange, and API gateways and application workloads to issue and validate post-quantum-signed tokens.2 Hybrid key exchange, which adds ML-KEM to today’s elliptic-curve exchange, is on by default in major browsers and common TLS libraries, yet in early 2026 only about one in ten origin servers could use it.9 The server-to-server traffic that agents generate therefore lags behind browser traffic. Research on AI infrastructure by the Cloud Security Alliance recommends upgrading the TLS between services in agentic architectures, starting with hybrid key exchange.10

5. Audits, cryptographic discovery and board action

Boards should ask to see a maintained inventory of where cryptography is used and a record of migration progress, and the action plan they oversee runs in four stages.11,12

  1. Inventory. Scan applications, data stores, APIs and agent traffic endpoints to find where asymmetric encryption protects data today.
  2. Prioritize. Secure the most critical business functions, high-value financial transactions and long-lived data first.
  3. Migrate in phases. Adopt the NIST ML-KEM and ML-DSA standards,5 and build crypto-agility into API gateways and cloud boundary controls, so that algorithms can be swapped without changing core code.
  4. Govern. Treat quantum readiness as an enterprise-wide program with formal board oversight and cross-functional coordination, built into governance, risk and compliance processes.11,12 Name a migration lead and report progress to the board on a regular cadence, for example quarterly.

6. How AI shortens the path to cryptanalysis

CIOs and CTOs should not count on years of slack, because AI is shortening the time between finding a cryptographic weakness and exploiting it.

In July 2026, Anthropic reported that its Claude Mythos Preview model, working largely autonomously, found new mathematical attacks on a weakened, seven-round version of the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) and on HAWK, a candidate post-quantum signature scheme still under NIST evaluation.13,14 The AES attack is 200 to 800 times faster than the best earlier human-developed attack on that weakened cipher, though it remains far from practical.13

Anthropic stated that these findings do not threaten the encryption standards deployed today.13,14 In separate tests in June 2026, the same model turned newly patched software flaws into working exploits within hours, work that has historically taken days to weeks.15,16

7. Governance gaps

Corporate readiness is low, and that is a regulatory and operational risk. A 2026 board discussion guide in the Director’s Handbook on Cyber-Risk Oversight, published by the National Association of Corporate Directors and the Internet Security Alliance, cites Bain research finding that roughly 90% of companies are unprepared for quantum threats, with most executives reporting no plan and no budget for the transition.12,11 It also cites an ISACA study finding that only 4% of organizations have a defined quantum strategy.12

CIOs and CTOs who start cryptographic discovery now can make quantum readiness a point of difference, and part of the case that their systems can be trusted.
