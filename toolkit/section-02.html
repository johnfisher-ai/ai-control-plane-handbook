Section 2

Framing the use case and the value hypothesis

Framing decides everything that follows. The goal of the first workshop is one use case with a named sponsor and business owner, a clear trigger and end state, a scope small enough to start in 90 days, and a value hypothesis the business owner believes enough to measure.

Choosing the use case

Score each candidate from 1 to 5 against the criteria below, with evidence. As a rule of thumb, a strong first use case scores 28 or more out of 35, and nothing below 3 on a safe first step or reachable systems. Fix those first or choose another candidate.

CriterionWhat good looks like
Value at stakeVolume times the time, money or risk per item is large enough to matter to the sponsor.
Visible painThe people doing the work, and their leaders, already feel the delay, cost or risk.
Reachable systemsThe systems of record have APIs or connectors, or can get them within the first phase.
Data readinessThe master data the agents need exists and can be made trustworthy.
Safe first stepThe first phase can run read-only or propose-only, with people keeping the decisions.
Measurable in 90 daysA baseline exists or can be taken now, and the first slice can show movement within a quarter.
Sponsor and ownerAn executive sponsor and a business owner are named and available.

Describing the use case at a glance

Capture the use case in the client’s words, on one page (template T1):

  • Problem today. The chain of manual, risk-sensitive steps and what the delays and errors cost.
  • Target state. What the agents will do, what the people will keep and which systems are involved.
  • Trigger and end state. The event that starts the process and what is true when it is done.
  • Users and systems of record. Who takes part, and the systems the process runs on.
  • Out of scope. What the first phase excludes. Saying it early prevents scope creep later.
  • Stages. Five to seven stages from trigger to end state, each with its human step, its work mode (human-led, AI-assisted or AI-led), its agent and autonomy tier, and its system of record.
  • Goals. Business goals the sponsor will judge success by, and control-plane goals: governed traffic, cost per outcome, evaluation before promotion and oversight people can exercise.

Deciding which work stays human

Decide which work should stay human before deciding how to automate the rest. BCG suggests asking two questions of every step: could AI do it as well as or better than people, and should it, given regulatory risk, customer trust, ethics and safety?85 The answers sort each stage into one of three work modes (T1):

Work modeWhat it meansTypical stepsTiers and controls
Human-led
  • A person does the work and decides
  • AI may inform
Steps that carry professional, regulatory or safety accountability, or where trust depends on a personTier 1 at most: agents assemble and check, and a person decides
AI-assisted
  • AI drafts, proposes or checks
  • A person reviews and accepts
Judgment work with clear criteria and a reviewer who can check the resultTiers 1 and 2: drafts and proposals, with bound approvals for anything that writes
AI-led
  • Agents do the work within bounds
  • People supervise and handle exceptions
High-volume, rule-based work whose results can be checked and reversedTiers 2 and 3: limits, monitoring, a kill switch and a takeover runbook

Ask the should-AI questions of every stage an agent could do:

  • Regulatory risk: does a law, regulator or professional standard expect a person to decide?
  • Customer trust: would the customer or client expect a person here?
  • Ethics: could the decision treat people unfairly, and could anyone explain it?
  • Safety: could an error harm people, assets or the environment, and can it be reversed?

Record the reason for every human-led step, since it is what risk teams and boards ask about first. Record the split before any decision on roles, too: Deloitte’s 2026 Human Capital Trends found that 57% of leaders who cut staff because of AI have come to question that decision.87

Writing the value hypothesis

Write the business case as a small set of value levers, each with a formula the business owner and finance can check (template T2). Use the client’s volumes and rates, keep the assumptions conservative, and show the arithmetic.

Value leverWhat it measuresFormula
Capacity releasedHours of manual work removed, valued at loaded costVolume × hours saved × loaded cost per hour
Revenue protected or acceleratedRevenue that is no longer lost, written off or delayedVolume affected × revenue per item recovered or pulled forward
Utilization and throughputMore output from the same people or assetsPoints of utilization × people × hours × rate
Cost avoidedPenalties, fails, outages, rework or external spend avoidedEvents avoided × cost per event
Risk avoidedRegulatory, compliance, safety or reputational exposure reduced
  • Usually not quantified
  • Describe and evidence it
  • Show one formula per lever, with its inputs and their source.
  • Separate run-rate value (a year at full scale) from one-time value. The plan phases the run rate.
  • Quote values before platform, model and change costs until the project team has estimated them. Then show net value and payback.
  • Describe value that is hard to quantify, such as risk avoided, in words and evidence, without inventing a number.
  • Set the case against published payback benchmarks, and judge generative and agentic slices on separate horizons (Section 6).
  • Label every figure illustrative until the baseline is measured and finance agrees the method.
