Section 8

The 12- to 24-month plan: value at every phase

Adopt the reference architecture in phases, and expect returns in every phase. The concern is well founded. In a survey of more than 4,400 chief executives published in January 2026, 56% said AI had delivered no significant financial benefit to date;79 in a mid-2026 survey, 37% of respondents attributed any EBIT impact to AI;80 and many agentic AI projects are expected to be canceled over cost, unclear value or weak risk controls.6 The same research shows what the organizations that capture value do differently. They build strong foundations, which makes meaningful financial returns three times more likely.79 They build “a repeatable capability” instead of “simply accumulating tools or pilots,” and they redesign workflows around it.80 And they start small, embedding AI in adjacent processes, “demonstrating clear value, then scaling into core workflows,” with top performers moving from pilot to full implementation in about 90 days.81

This plan follows that pattern. The reference architecture is adopted incrementally: each phase switches on part of the control plane and, in the same window, puts a slice of a real use case into production, so value arrives from the first milestone while the platform matures. Because each phase proves its value before the next is funded, the program can be approved in stages rather than as a single 24-month commitment, and its return is measured against actual platform, model and change costs as it goes.

The next 12 to 24 months are the time to architect the controls, not to finish the agentic transition.1 Sequence matters. The plan below establishes visibility and a single policy point first, then unlocks capabilities and routing, then hardens trust, and federates last; published phasing guidance likewise starts with governance, a single policy point and baseline guardrails, and federates only at scale.20

Value at every milestone

Each phase ends in a milestone the business can see. The architecture delivers value of its own, from spend visibility to faster delivery of every new agent, and each milestone brings more of a real use case into production. Report the evidence in the right-hand column at every steering review; it feeds the scorecard in Section 9.

Set these milestones against published payback data, because finance will. In Deloitte’s 2025 survey of 1,854 executives in Europe and the Middle East, most achieved satisfactory ROI on a typical AI use case within two to four years, against the seven to 12 months expected of technology investments; only 6% saw payback within a year, and just 10% were realizing significant ROI from agentic AI.82 Expectations are shortening: in KPMG’s 2025 Global CEO Outlook, 67% of 1,350 CEOs expected returns on AI within one to three years, where a year earlier most expected three to five.83 Google Cloud’s 2026 survey of 2,403 executives found 84% seeing increasing financial returns from AI and 26% seeing returns accelerate year over year.84 Phasing is how a program beats the benchmark: read-only and propose-only slices release capacity within months and help fund the controls that the larger gains depend on. The leaders in Deloitte’s survey judge generative and agentic AI differently, with 85% using different frameworks or timeframes for each;82 judge the early slices on months, and the agentic ones that act in systems of record on a longer horizon.

Phasing in the use cases

The same pattern works inside each use case: start read-only in one scope, prove the value, widen the scope, then let agents act in systems of record once the controls from phase 3 are in place. Two patterns hold across all three. The early milestones release capacity from assembling, checking and drafting, which agents can do safely in read-only or propose-only modes. The larger gains, such as revenue protected by timely charge codes, automated settlement fixes and avoided outages, arrive once agents can act in systems of record under the approvals and limits that phase 3 puts in place. Sequencing each use case with the architecture keeps every step both valuable and safe.

Operating model: owners, decision rights and skills

Technology is the smaller part of the work. BCG estimates that becoming AI-first “involves 30% technology and 70% people and organization.”85 In Microsoft’s 2026 Work Trend Index, a survey of 20,000 people using AI at work in 10 countries, only one in four said their leadership is clearly and consistently aligned on AI, and organizational factors accounted for more than twice the reported impact of individual ones.86 The organizations whose returns are accelerating set ownership deliberately: in Google Cloud’s survey, 48% of them had “extremely clear” ownership and decision rights for AI and agentic initiatives, and 38% built required AI training into roles.84 The plan’s accountable executive and governance council need a day-to-day model beneath them:

Decide which work should stay human before deciding how to automate the rest. BCG suggests sorting work into human-led, AI-assisted and AI-led by asking two questions of each step: could AI do it as well as or better than people, and should it, given regulatory risk, customer trust, ethics and safety.85 Record the answer before any decision on roles: Deloitte’s 2026 Human Capital Trends found that 57% of leaders who cut staff because of AI have come to question that decision.87 Build skills in step with the plan: approvers trained on what they approve (Section 4), sponsors on the agent lifecycle (Section 2), and business teams on working alongside agents.

The north star reached: all goals met

By the end of phase 4 the target reference architecture from Section 6 is in place, and every goal set at the start is met:

  • Architecture. Every agent is registered with an owner and a tier; all model, MCP and A2A traffic passes one policy point; the top business capabilities are reusable by any agent; and discovery and orchestration are federated across domains.
  • Trust. Every production agent has an identity, a tier and an evaluation baseline; high-risk actions execute exactly as approved; drills show that people can stop an agent in time; and post-quantum migration is on track for 2030.
  • Value. Each use case runs at its full run rate, with its KPI targets met since month 12. For the illustrative use cases, cumulative value by month 24 is about $33 million, $12 million and $3.8 million, before platform, model and change costs.
  • Economics. Cost per outcome is falling, and models can be swapped without application changes when prices or providers change.

The first 90 days

  • Appoint an accountable owner and an AI governance council spanning architecture, security, data and the business.
  • Run Agent Scanners and register every known agent, API and MCP server in Agent Registry with an owner, a sponsor and a risk tier.
  • Route new LLM and MCP traffic through AI Gateway; baseline tokens, cost and data flows by use case.
  • Publish autonomy tiers, a policy stack and an exception process.29
  • Identify the 10 to 20 business capabilities agents most need, and plan their APIs and MCP tools.
  • Start a cryptographic inventory and name a post-quantum migration lead.
  • Pick the first use-case slice, baseline its KPIs with finance and agree how its value will be reported.
